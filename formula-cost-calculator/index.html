How Much Does Baby Formula Cost?

Published by Radif Partners · Updated 21 September 2026

Baby formula costs between $55 and $250 a month depending almost entirely on which brand you buy, and the composition of every infant formula sold in the United States must meet the same federally mandated nutrient requirements regardless of price. A store brand at $0.56 to $0.71 an ounce and a premium brand at $1.75 to $1.79 an ounce are both regulated to that standard. Over twelve months the difference is $900 to $1,400 for a formula-fed baby. Consumption rises from roughly 20 ounces a day at one month to a peak near 32 ounces around five or six months, then falls as solid food is introduced, which is why monthly cost is not flat: expect the highest bills between months four and eight.

Ready-to-feed vs powder

Formula comes in three forms, and the price differences are dramatic:

  • Powder: Cheapest option at $0.56-$1.79/oz depending on brand. Requires mixing with water. Annual cost: $700-$2,800.
  • Concentrated liquid: Middle ground. Mix with equal parts water. About 1.5x the cost of powder. Annual cost: $1,200-$4,000.
  • Ready-to-feed: Most convenient, no mixing needed. Perfect for travel and nighttime feeds. But 2-3x the cost of powder. Annual cost: $2,500-$6,000.

Most families use powder at home and keep ready-to-feed bottles in the diaper bag for outings.

How to save on formula

  • Switch to store brands: Kirkland (Costco), Parent's Choice (Walmart), and Up & Up (Target) all meet the same FDA nutritional standards as Enfamil and Similac. Annual savings: $700-$1,000.
  • Buy in bulk: Costco and Sam's Club offer the lowest per-ounce prices. A Costco membership ($65/year) pays for itself in 2 months of formula savings.
  • Sign up for manufacturer rewards: Enfamil Family Beginnings and Similac StrongMoms send free samples and coupons. Sign up for both during pregnancy.
  • Check WIC eligibility: The WIC program covers formula for families up to 185% of the federal poverty level (about $57,720 for a family of 4). WIC covers about half of all formula sold in the US.
  • Amazon Subscribe & Save: 5-15% off with automatic delivery.
  • Never buy from unofficial resellers. Formula from unknown third parties may be expired, counterfeit, or improperly stored.

The breastfeeding vs formula financial comparison

The honest math on both options:

Breastfeeding costs

  • Breast pump: $0 (often covered by insurance in the US) to $300
  • Storage bags: $80-$150/year
  • Nursing bras: $50-$100
  • Nursing pads: $30-$60/year
  • Total: $0-$600/year

Formula costs

  • Store brand powder: $700-$1,000/year
  • Name brand powder: $1,200-$2,800/year
  • Organic/premium: $1,800-$3,500/year

Breastfeeding is cheaper financially. But time has value too, the hours spent pumping, cleaning parts, and managing supply are real costs that don't show up on a spreadsheet. There's no wrong choice here. Feed your baby in the way that works for your family.

A full year of exclusive formula feeding therefore costs roughly $850 to $1,100 on a store brand and $2,200 to $2,700 on a premium one. Mixed feeding, where formula supplements breastfeeding, typically runs at 40 to 60 per cent of those figures.

What you are paying for above the standard

Every infant formula sold in the United States must meet the nutrient requirements set in federal regulation and is subject to FDA oversight. That covers the macronutrient profile, the vitamin and mineral content, and the manufacturing controls. A store brand cannot legally fall below it, and a premium brand cannot meaningfully exceed it on those measures.

What premium brands add sits outside the mandated floor: specific added ingredients such as particular HMO oligosaccharides or lutein blends, organic certification, a different protein source such as goat or A2 milk, or a hydrolysed protein for suspected sensitivity. Some of these matter for a specific baby on medical advice. None of them matter for the average baby, and the FDA does not permit a claim that one brand produces better outcomes than another on the basis of them. If a paediatrician has recommended a specialised formula, buy it; if nobody has, the store brand is the same category of product.

Powder, concentrate and ready-to-feed

  • Powder is the cheapest by a wide margin, roughly $0.56 to $1.79 an ounce of prepared formula depending on brand. It requires safe preparation with water at the correct temperature.
  • Liquid concentrate costs roughly 20 to 40 per cent more than powder and removes one step. It is a reasonable middle option for night feeds.
  • Ready-to-feed costs two to three times powder. It is sterile, which makes it the recommended form for newborns under two months, premature babies and any infant with a compromised immune system, and it is the sensible choice for travel. Using it routinely for a healthy older baby adds $1,500 or more over a year.

Ways to reduce the cost that do not compromise anything

  1. Switch to a store brand. The largest single saving available, $900 to $1,400 a year, with no change in the regulated composition. Switch gradually over a week if the baby is established on another brand.
  2. Buy the largest container size. Unit cost falls 15 to 25 per cent between a 20-ounce tin and a 42-ounce one.
  3. Check WIC eligibility. The programme provides formula directly, and income thresholds are higher than most families assume.
  4. Ask the paediatrician for samples. Practices receive them routinely, and for a specialised formula this can be worth several hundred dollars.
  5. Use manufacturer programmes for specialised formula. Where a hypoallergenic or amino-acid formula is medically necessary, both the manufacturer's programme and, in many cases, insurance coverage can apply. Ask for a letter of medical necessity.
  6. Do not stockpile beyond a month. Tins have expiry dates, babies switch stages, and a change of formula on medical advice makes a large stock a loss rather than a saving.

Things to avoid

Do not dilute formula to make it last longer. It is the most dangerous economy in infant feeding and can cause water intoxication, seizures and failure to thrive. Do not make formula at home from any recipe, whatever its provenance. Do not buy formula from informal resale channels, where storage conditions, expiry dates and authenticity cannot be verified; during the 2022 shortage this was the route by which counterfeit and expired product reached families. Do not use toddler milks marketed for children over twelve months as a cheaper substitute for infant formula, they are a different product, are not regulated to the infant standard, and are not appropriate under one year.

Imported European formulas occupy a grey area. They are regulated in their home markets to standards that are broadly comparable but not identical, and most are not FDA-reviewed for the American market, so labelling and preparation instructions may differ from what a caregiver expects. They are also among the most expensive options available, which means the trade-off is not a saving in any case.

Formula versus breastfeeding, honestly costed

Breastfeeding is frequently described as free, and it is not. A double electric pump costs $150 to $400, though most American insurance plans are required to cover one and many families buy one anyway without checking. Bottles, storage bags, breast pads, nipple cream and a nursing bra or two add $150 to $300. Where a mother returns to work, pumping requires either a second pump for the office or carrying one daily, and the time it takes is real even where the law protects the break.

Against that, exclusive breastfeeding avoids $850 to $2,700 of formula over a year, so the net saving is genuine and substantial: roughly $600 to $2,300. The honest framing is that breastfeeding is much cheaper but not free, that mixed feeding sits proportionally in between, and that the difference between a store brand and a premium brand is larger than many families realise, a formula-fed baby on a store brand costs less than the pump-and-bottle setup plus a premium brand.

Cost should not be the deciding factor in how a baby is fed, and for many families it is not a choice at all. It belongs in the budget as a range of $0 to $2,700 with a most-likely figure around $900, rather than as a line assumed to be zero.

Planning the formula line in a first-year budget

Three practical points make this line predictable. First, decide the brand before the baby is born, and buy one tin rather than a case, because a proportion of babies do not tolerate the first formula tried and a stocked case becomes a donation. Second, expect the peak months to cost 40 to 60 per cent more than the first, so a budget built on month-one spending will be short by roughly $300 over the year. Third, the line does not end at twelve months in the way families assume: whole milk replaces formula from one year, at a fraction of the cost, but the transition takes several weeks and overlaps.

For a mixed-feeding household, the most useful planning figure is 50 per cent of the exclusive-formula cost for the brand chosen, reviewed at three months when the actual pattern is established. Families who budget for exclusive breastfeeding and end up mixed feeding are the ones most often caught out, and the gap is $400 to $1,300 depending on brand.

Costs outside the United States

Formula is cheaper almost everywhere else, and the gap is larger than exchange rates explain. In the United Kingdom, first-infant formula runs £9 to £14 for an 800 g tin, which works out at roughly £45 to £90 a month, and advertising restrictions on infant formula mean brands compete less on marketing and more on price. Retailers are also prohibited from applying loyalty discounts or promotions to first-infant formula, so the shelf price is the price everywhere.

In the UAE, a tin costs AED 45 to AED 95 depending on brand and origin, with European imports at the top of that range and regional brands at the bottom, giving a monthly cost of roughly AED 180 to AED 400. Across the European Union, compositional standards are set at union level and the equivalent of a store brand is widely available at 40 to 60 per cent of premium prices, exactly as in the United States.

The practical lesson for a family moving between countries is that the brand they are established on may be unavailable or differently formulated on arrival, and that the transition is easier planned than discovered. Where a specialised formula is medically necessary, check availability before the move rather than after it, and carry a few weeks' supply.

Frequently asked questions

How much does baby formula cost per month?
Baby formula costs $55-$250 per month depending on brand. Store brands like Kirkland (Costco) cost $55-$100/month, Consumption peaks around four to five months at roughly 32 ounces a day, then falls as solids start, so the monthly bill is highest in the middle of the first year rather than at the beginning.
Is store brand formula as good as name brand?
Yes. All infant formula sold in the US must meet the same FDA nutritional standards. Store brands like Kirkland, Parent's Choice, and Up & Up are nutritionally equivalent to Enfamil and Similac at 30-50% lower cost. What differs between brands is the protein source and the added ingredients, which matter for a diagnosed intolerance and not otherwise.
How much formula does a baby need per day?
Roughly 2.5 ounces per pound of body weight per day for the first six months, so a 12 lb baby takes about 30 ounces. Consumption peaks around four to five months at 30 to 32 ounces daily, then falls as solids are introduced. A 12.5 oz can of powder makes about 90 fluid ounces, which is three days for a baby at peak intake.
Is ready-to-feed formula worth the extra cost?
It costs roughly two to three times powder per ounce, so a month on ready-to-feed can add $150 to $250. Its case is convenience and sterility rather than nutrition: it is genuinely useful for newborns, for travel, for night feeds and for immunocompromised infants. Most families use it selectively and powder for the rest, which is where the budget lands.
Can I get help paying for formula?
In the United States, WIC covers formula for eligible families and serves roughly half of all infants, with income limits around 185 per cent of the poverty line. Many manufacturers run rebate and sample programmes worth $100 to $200 over the first year. Hospitals and paediatric practices distribute samples that are worth asking for rather than waiting to be offered.
Why did formula prices rise so much?
The 2022 shortage that followed a major plant closure reset prices across the category, and they have not fully returned. Concentration is the underlying reason: a small number of manufacturers supply most of the US market, and WIC contracts award near-exclusivity state by state. Store brands, which must meet the same FDA nutrient standards, are the practical response.
