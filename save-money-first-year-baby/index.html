How to Cut Baby Costs by 40% in the First Year

Published by Radif Partners · Updated 21 September 2026

A first-year baby budget can be cut by 30 to 40 per cent without buying anything worse, because most of what a new family spends goes on items that lose their value the moment they leave the shop and keep almost all of their usefulness. Buying durable equipment second-hand saves $800 to $2,000. Switching to a store-brand formula saves $700 to $1,000 a year for the same regulated composition. Reusable nappies save $400 to $1,200 over two years. Claiming every tax provision you are entitled to returns $1,000 to $3,000. Together these come to $3,000 to $7,000 in the first twelve months, and none of them involves doing without. The three things worth paying full price for, a new car seat, a safe sleep surface and good medical cover, are covered at the end.

Strategy 1: Buy gear secondhand (save $800–$2,000)

A new stroller, crib, dresser, high chair, and baby carrier can easily cost $2,000–$4,000. The same items in good condition on Facebook Marketplace, OfferUp, or consignment shops: $400–$1,200.

Safe to buy used: Strollers, clothing, toys, bouncer seats, baby carriers, high chairs, dressers, changing tables, bottles, play mats.

Never buy used: Car seats (invisible damage, expiration dates), cribs manufactured before June 2011 (safety standards changed), helmets, breast pumps.

Pro tip: Post in local parent groups that you're looking for specific items. Parents love passing things on, many will give items free or cheap.

Check the CPSC recall database before buying any used gear.

“We furnished our entire nursery for $600 using Facebook Marketplace. The Uppababy stroller we got for $150 retails for $900.”

Strategy 2: Switch to store-brand formula (save $700–$1,000/year)

All infant formula in the US must meet the same FDA nutritional standards, by law. Store brands cost 30–50% less than name brands:

  • Kirkland (Costco): $0.71/oz vs Enfamil NeuroPro at $1.79/oz
  • Parent's Choice (Walmart): $0.61/oz
  • Up & Up (Target): $0.56/oz

At 25–32 oz/day (typical 3–6 month baby), that's a difference of $25–$35/week. Annual savings: $700–$1,000 switching from Enfamil/Similac to store brand.

Also: sign up for Enfamil Family Beginnings AND Similac StrongMoms during pregnancy for free samples and coupons worth $50–$100 each.

“We used Kirkland formula for both kids. Identical ingredients, identical nutrition, half the price.”

Strategy 3: Use cloth diapers or a diaper service (save $400–$1,200/year)

Disposable diapers: $800–$1,200/year (8–12 diapers/day at $0.25–$0.35 each).

Cloth diaper startup: $200–$500 for a full set of 24–30 diapers. Then just laundry costs (~$100–$150/year).

Diaper service (they wash for you): $60–$90/month ($720–$1,080/year), still saves vs premium disposables.

Not ready for full-time cloth? Use cloth at home and disposables when out. Even 50% cloth saves $300–$500/year.

If sticking with disposables: buy store brand (Costco Kirkland, Amazon Mama Bear), subscribe & save for 5–15% off, and stock up during sales.

“The cloth diaper learning curve is real, give yourself 2 weeks. After that, it becomes routine.”

Strategy 4: Breastfeed if you can (save $1,000–$2,500/year)

Formula costs $700–$2,800/year depending on brand. Breastfeeding costs: $0–$600/year (pump covered by insurance, plus bags, pads, bras). Net savings: $700–$2,200/year.

Important caveats:

  • “If you can” is the key phrase. Not all mothers can or want to breastfeed, and that's fine.
  • Hidden costs of breastfeeding: your time (8–12 sessions/day initially), potential lactation consultant ($150–$300), nipple cream, storage bags.
  • Combination feeding (breast milk + formula) is common and still saves money.

Check that your insurance covers a breast pump (ACA requires it). Spectra and Medela are popular insurance-covered options.

Free milk storage hack: freeze in ice cube trays, then transfer to bags, uses fewer bags.

Strategy 5: Accept (and ask for) hand-me-downs (save $300–$800)

Babies outgrow clothing every 2–3 months for the first year. That's 4–5 complete wardrobe turnovers.

New baby clothing budget: $500–$1,200/year. Hand-me-down budget: $0–$200/year.

Most baby clothes are worn 10–15 times before being outgrown, they're practically new.

How to get them: tell everyone you know that you welcome hand-me-downs. Join local “Buy Nothing” groups. Check church and community rummage sales.

What to buy new: car seat (safety), mattress (hygiene/safety), shoes when they start walking (fit matters), seasonal outerwear if timing doesn't match hand-me-downs.

“Our second child wore almost exclusively hand-me-downs for the first 18 months. Total clothing cost: under $100.”

Strategy 6: Maximize tax benefits (save $1,000–$3,000/year)

  • Child Tax Credit: $2,000 per child (up to $1,700 refundable)
  • Dependent Care FSA: $5,000 pre-tax for childcare = $1,000–$1,500 in tax savings
  • Health FSA/HSA: use pre-tax dollars for birth costs, pediatrician visits, prescriptions
  • Some states add their own credits (California $1,117, Colorado $1,200, Minnesota $1,750)

“The CTC alone is worth $167/month. The FSA saves another $100/month in taxes. That's $267/month most new parents leave on the table.”

You won't use every strategy. Breastfeeding and formula savings don't fully stack, for instance. But even picking 3–4 of these saves $3,000–$6,000 in the first year. That's 30–45% off the average first-year cost.

What NOT to cheap out on

  • Car seat: Buy new. Non-negotiable. Don't accept used car seats, you can't verify their history. Budget $150–$350.
  • Crib mattress: Buy new for hygiene and safety. $80–$200.
  • Health insurance: Don't skip well-baby visits or vaccinations to save money. Most are covered at 100% under ACA.
  • Safe sleep setup: A firm mattress in a bare crib. No bumpers, blankets, or positioners. This costs less, not more, than the Instagram nursery.

The order to do this in

Savings are not equal in effort or in size, and families who start with the small ones usually run out of patience before reaching the large ones. In descending order of return per hour spent:

  1. Set up the pre-tax accounts. A dependent care FSA and an HSA take twenty minutes at open enrolment and return $2,000 to $4,000 a year. Nothing else on this list comes close on that basis.
  2. Sort out childcare before you need it. A place booked three months ahead is cheaper and better than one taken in a hurry, and the difference between two providers a mile apart is frequently $200 a month.
  3. Buy the big equipment used. One weekend of marketplace browsing covers the cot, the pushchair, the carrier and the high chair for a quarter of retail.
  4. Fix the recurring items. Formula brand, nappy source and subscription settings are decided once and then run for a year.
  5. Everything else. Clothing, toys and books are where families spend the most effort saving the least. Accept hand-me-downs and stop optimising.

The recurring costs are where the year is decided

One-off purchases feel expensive and are not the main event. Twelve months of nappies at $75 a month is $900; twelve months of formula at $150 a month is $1,800. Those two lines alone outweigh the entire equipment list for most families, which is why a decision made once about where they come from matters more than any individual bargain.

On formula, the composition of infant formula is regulated to a minimum standard, so a store brand meeting that standard is nutritionally equivalent to a premium one at a third of the price. On nappies, the cheapest reliable route is usually a subscription from a large retailer with the delivery frequency set slightly too low and topped up as needed, rather than the frequency the subscription suggests. On wipes, buying the largest case available roughly halves the unit cost. None of this requires ongoing attention once set.

Free money most families miss

  • A breast pump through insurance. Most American plans are required to cover one, and many families buy one anyway without checking.
  • WIC and equivalent programmes. Income thresholds are higher than most people assume, and a household that did not qualify before the birth may qualify after it.
  • Hospital discharge supplies. Nappies, wipes, formula samples and a nasal aspirator are already billed to the room. Take them.
  • Manufacturer parenting clubs. Free samples and coupons worth $50 to $150 over the first year, at the cost of an email address used for nothing else.
  • Library and toy-library membership. Board books and toys for ages that last three months each.
  • Employer benefits. Childcare backup days, adoption or fertility allowances and baby bonuses are common and under-claimed because they are listed in a handbook nobody reads.

A worked example

A first-time couple with a household income of $95,000 in a mid-cost state. They buy the cot, pushchair, carrier and high chair second-hand for $340 instead of $1,500, and a new car seat and mattress for $290. They use a store-brand formula from month four, at $95 a month rather than $165. They start a dependent care FSA at $3,750 and an HSA at the family limit. They claim the Child Tax Credit and take the free pump through insurance.

Against a no-optimisation baseline of about $23,000 for the year, they spend roughly $16,800, a saving of $6,200, of which $3,600 comes from the two tax accounts and the pump, $1,160 from buying used, and the rest from the recurring lines. They bought nothing of lower quality except the formula, which is the same product with a different label.

Savings that are not worth the effort

A guide that only lists what to do is half a guide. Three widely repeated tips cost more in time and stress than they return, and skipping them deliberately is part of the plan.

  • Extreme couponing on nappies. Hours of clipping and stacking for $10 to $20 a month, at the point in life when spare hours are scarcest. Setting a subscription once captures most of the same discount permanently.
  • Making your own baby food from the start. The saving is real but small, around $15 to $25 a month once you count the equipment and the waste, and it arrives at six months when the household has the least capacity for a new weekly routine. It becomes worthwhile later, if at all.
  • Hunting for the cheapest possible pushchair. A bad one gets replaced within a year, which costs more than buying a good used one at the outset. Buy the model that was expensive three years ago, second-hand.

The principle behind all three is the same: prefer a decision made once that keeps paying, over a habit that must be maintained. A subscription, a tax election and a well-chosen used purchase all keep working while you are asleep. Coupons, batch cooking and weekly price comparisons all stop the moment the week gets difficult, which in the first year is most weeks. Set the durable decisions up once, in the second trimester if you can, and let the rest go. The households that save the most are not the ones that try hardest each week; they are the ones that made five good decisions early and then stopped thinking about it.

Frequently asked questions

How can I save money on baby formula?
Switch to store brands (Kirkland, Parent's Choice, Up & Up). They meet the same FDA nutritional standards as Enfamil and Similac at 30-50% lower cost. Annual savings: $700-$1,000. Also sign up for manufacturer rewards programs for free samples and coupons.
Is it safe to buy secondhand baby gear?
Yes, for most items. Strollers, clothing, toys, high chairs, and baby carriers are safe to buy used. Never buy secondhand car seats (may be expired or crash-damaged), cribs manufactured before 2011 (safety standards changed), or breast pumps (hygiene). Check CPSC recalls before buying anything used.
What is genuinely worth buying new?
Three things: the car seat, the mattress and anything that has been recalled. A car seat has an expiry date, usually six to ten years from manufacture, and its crash history is unknowable second-hand. A mattress should be firm and undamaged for safe sleep. Everything else, cot, pushchair, clothes, carrier, changing table, holds up perfectly well bought used.
How much can second-hand buying actually save?
Between $1,000 and $2,500 over the first year for most families. Baby clothes are worn for weeks, so a bundle of sleepsuits at a tenth of retail is common. Pushchairs and cots depreciate 60 to 80 per cent in a year. The saving is largest in the first six months, when growth is fastest and items are outgrown before they wear out.
Which recurring costs are easiest to reduce?
Nappies and formula, in that order, because they are bought every week for two years. Store-brand nappies cost 30 to 40 per cent less than the leading brands and perform comparably in independent testing. Store-brand formula must meet identical FDA nutrient standards and costs 30 to 50 per cent less. Together they are worth $600 to $1,200 a year.
Is a baby registry worth setting up?
Yes, and less for the gifts than for the completion discount: most retailers give 10 to 20 per cent off anything left on the list after the shower. Registering for a few large items rather than many small ones is what makes that discount meaningful. Cash funds for childcare or a college account are increasingly accepted and more useful than a fifth blanket.
What do parents most regret buying?
Surveys and resale listings point the same way: wipe warmers, bottle sterilisers, changing tables, baby shoes, a second pushchair and most single-purpose gadgets. The common thread is equipment bought before the baby arrived, for a routine that had not yet been established. Waiting until a need appears costs nothing and avoids most of the list.
Does breastfeeding actually save money?
It avoids the formula bill, which runs $660 to $3,000 in the first year, but it is not free. Pumps, bottles, storage bags, nursing bras and lactation consultant visits add $200 to $800, and the time cost is real for a parent returning to work. In the United States, insurance must cover a pump and lactation support, which most families never claim.
