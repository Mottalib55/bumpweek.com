Having a Baby in Dubai: What It Actually Costs

Published by Radif Partners · Updated 21 September 2026

A birth in Dubai costs between AED 8,000 and AED 45,000 depending on the hospital, and how much of that the family pays depends almost entirely on one document: the insurance policy. Health cover is mandatory for residents, but maternity benefit is not part of every employer plan, and where it is included it is typically capped at AED 10,000 to AED 15,000 with a co-payment of 10 to 20 per cent and a waiting period of six to twelve months from the start of the policy. Afterwards, there is no state child benefit of any kind, maternity leave is 45 days at full pay and 15 at half, and paternity leave is five working days. Childcare is cheap by Western standards and school fees are not, which inverts the usual expat budget.

This guide sets out what a birth in Dubai actually costs, using the published rates of the main hospitals and the budgets expat families report. It covers the package prices, the insurance rules that decide how much of them you pay, the childcare market afterwards, and the costs that are consistently left out of the estimate.

The hospital -- it's all about the package

If there's one thing you learn fast as an expecting parent in Dubai, it's that hospitals here run on packages. Think of it like a hotel bundle: you get a set price that covers delivery, a certain number of nights, and basic post-birth care. Everything beyond that is an add-on.

For a vaginal delivery, you're looking at AED 13,000 to 22,000 if you're paying out of pocket. With insurance covering part of it, expect to pay AED 5,000 to 12,000 depending on your plan. C-section packages run higher: AED 18,000 to 35,000 self-pay, or AED 8,000 to 18,000 with insurance picking up its share.

The major hospitals most expats end up choosing between are Mediclinic City Hospital (probably the most popular with Western expats), NMC Royal Women's Hospital, Al Zahra Hospital in Sharjah, and Danat Al Emarat in Abu Dhabi. The choice is usually driven by two things: where the obstetrician already practises, and the standard of the neonatal unit attached to the hospital, which is the facility a family hopes never to need and should nonetheless check before booking.

A standard package typically includes one to two nights' stay, a pediatrician check for the baby, and basic lab work. What it does not include is what really adds up. The epidural upgrade runs AED 3,000 to 5,000 on top of the package price. A private room upgrade costs extra. Each additional night in hospital costs more. And if there are any complications -- even minor ones -- the extras pile up fast.

My advice: get the package price in writing, then add 30% to your mental budget. That's closer to what you'll actually pay.

The insurance game

Here's the thing about health insurance in Dubai that trips up a lot of expat couples: most employer-provided health insurance does cover maternity. But there's almost always a waiting period, and if you don't know about it in advance, you can end up paying full price for delivery.

The most common waiting period is 12 months from the start of your policy. That means if you joined your company in March and conceived in June, your delivery in the following March might just barely squeak in -- or might not, depending on how your insurer counts the months. Families miss coverage by a matter of days often enough that the exact wording is worth confirming in writing before conceiving, not after.

Most policies that do cover maternity will pay around 80% of costs up to a cap. That cap is usually somewhere between AED 15,000 and 30,000. Sounds generous until you realize a complicated delivery can easily blow past AED 30,000.

My strongest advice for anyone planning a pregnancy in Dubai: check your insurance policy before you conceive. Read the maternity section. Call the insurer directly and ask them to confirm your coverage start date and your cap in writing. If you're switching jobs, know that you must disclose a pre-existing pregnancy to your new insurer -- and they may not cover it under the new policy.

This is the most expensive assumption expat families make. Maternity cover is not automatic on a basic employer policy, and where it exists it carries a ceiling and a co-payment. A family that assumes full coverage and meets a 20 per cent co-payment on a AED 40,000 delivery finds AED 8,000 on the invoice that was never in the budget.

Nanny vs nursery -- the Dubai equation

This is the decision that dominates every expat parent WhatsApp group in Dubai, and for good reason -- it's your single biggest ongoing expense after housing.

A live-in nanny in Dubai typically costs AED 2,500 to 4,500 per month in salary. But salary is just the start. You'll spend around AED 7,000 on her visa and medical insurance. You're providing food and accommodation (she lives with you). You'll need to budget for an annual flight home, which is a legal requirement. All in, a live-in nanny costs AED 42,000 to 66,000 per year when you factor in visa, insurance, flights, and salary.

A live-out nanny -- someone who comes to your home during work hours but doesn't live with you -- costs AED 4,000 to 7,000 per month. Higher salary because she's covering her own accommodation.

Nursery prices in Dubai range from AED 2,500 to 5,000 per month. Abu Dhabi tends to be slightly lower. Most nurseries don't take babies under 3 or 4 months old, so you'll need a solution for the gap between when maternity leave ends and nursery starts.

Most expat families in Dubai use nannies, and it's not considered a luxury here the way it might be in London or New York. It's culturally the norm and, when you do the math, often cheaper than nursery -- especially if you have more than one child.

The pattern most families settle into is a nanny under the age of two, where one-to-one care, flexible nap schedules and no fixed pick-up time matter most, followed by a nursery from two onwards for the social side. Running both at once, a nanny for the baby and a nursery place for the toddler, is expensive but common, and it is the arrangement that pushes a two-child household past AED 100,000 a year on childcare alone.

The costs nobody talks about

The hospital bill and childcare are the big numbers everyone asks about. But Dubai has a long tail of smaller costs that add up to a surprisingly large total.

Your child needs a dependent visa. That costs AED 3,000 to 5,000 annually, depending on your visa type and whether you use a typing center or go through your company's PRO. An Emirates ID for your child costs AED 370. These are non-negotiable -- your child literally cannot be in the country legally without them.

Pediatrician visits run AED 200 to 500 per visit. Some insurance plans cover well-baby checkups, but many don't cover them fully, and you'll be visiting the pediatrician a lot more often than you expect in the first year. Standard vaccinations are usually covered if you go to an in-network clinic, but if you end up at a private practice or need a vaccine outside the standard schedule, expect to pay AED 100 to 300 per shot.

Baby gear is noticeably more expensive in Dubai than in the US or UK. Strollers, car seats, cribs -- everything carries a 20 to 40% markup. That Uppababy stroller that costs $900 in the US? It's AED 4,500 here (roughly $1,225). Formula, diapers, and baby clothes are all marked up too, though less dramatically.

Then there's the leave situation. Maternity leave in the UAE is 60 days: 45 days at full pay, followed by 15 days at half pay. Paternity leave is 5 working days. That's it. There are no government child benefits, no child tax credits, no subsidies. Zero financial support from the state. If you're coming from the UK or a Scandinavian country, this can be a rude awakening.

What experienced expat families do differently

After two kids in Dubai, here's what I'd change if I were doing it again:

Buy gear online from the UK or US and ship it. Even with international shipping costs, a high-end stroller or car seat is cheaper to import than to buy at a Dubai mall. I've saved 25-35% doing this. Amazon UK ships to the UAE on many baby items, and the total including delivery still beats the local price.

Start the nanny search 3 months before your due date. We started 6 weeks before and it was incredibly stressful. Good nannies get snapped up fast, and the visa process takes 2-4 weeks on top of however long the search takes. Give yourself time to interview properly and check references.

Max out your insurance benefits. Use in-network hospitals and clinics exclusively. Every time you go out of network, your co-pay jumps and your cap gets eaten up faster. I know the boutique clinic in Jumeirah is lovely, but if it's out of network, it's not worth it financially.

Join Dubai expat parent groups. Facebook groups and WhatsApp communities are goldmines for secondhand gear. Expat families leave Dubai constantly, and they sell barely-used cribs, strollers, and baby clothes for a fraction of retail. We got our second crib for AED 200 -- it was practically new.

Budget AED 80,000 to 100,000 for the first year. That's a realistic range for a family using a nanny. If you're planning on nursery from the start, it might be slightly less. If you want premium everything, budget more. But AED 80,000 is a solid floor for a comfortable -- not luxurious -- first year with a baby in Dubai.

The insurance checklist, in order

Every avoidable cost on this page comes from one of the six points below. They are worth confirming in writing with the insurer, not the employer's HR department, because the policy wording governs.

  1. Is maternity included at all, and at what ceiling? Basic plans mandated for lower salary bands frequently exclude it.
  2. What is the waiting period, and from which date is it counted? Six or twelve months is standard, counted from the policy start rather than from employment.
  3. What is the co-payment, and does it apply to the whole bill or only to outpatient care? A 20 per cent co-payment on a caesarean is AED 6,000 to AED 9,000.
  4. Which hospitals are in the network at which tier? The same policy may cover a mid-tier hospital in full and a premium one at 50 per cent.
  5. Is antenatal care inside the maternity ceiling or separate? Scans and consultations across nine months run AED 6,000 to AED 12,000 and often eat into the delivery cap.
  6. How long is the window to add the newborn? Usually 30 days. After it closes the child is uninsured, and any neonatal care is billed privately at full rate.

Why the childcare maths is different here

In London or New York, a nanny is the most expensive childcare option by a wide margin. In Dubai it frequently is not. A live-out nanny costs AED 3,000 to AED 5,500 a month, a live-in one less in cash terms but with visa sponsorship, accommodation and medical cover added on top. A full-time nursery place runs AED 2,500 to AED 5,000 a month for one child, with no subsidy and no funded hours of any kind.

The crossover comes at the second child. Nursery fees are per child; a nanny's wage is not. Two children in nursery is AED 60,000 to AED 120,000 a year, while the same nanny covers both for what one child's nursery place would cost. This is the main reason the arrangement that looks extravagant to a newly arrived family is simply the default here, and it explains why the childcare line in a Dubai budget behaves so differently from the same line in a British or American one.

Planning around the leave rules

Sixty days of maternity leave, of which forty-five are at full pay, is the federal private-sector minimum, and free-zone authorities such as the DIFC and ADGM set their own more generous terms. Five working days of paternity leave applies across the private sector. There is no equivalent of shared parental leave, no state top-up, and no child benefit afterwards.

The practical consequence is that the return to work is early and the childcare arrangement must be in place before the baby is three months old. Families who wait until the end of maternity leave to look for a nanny or a nursery place regularly find the good options full, and pay a premium for what is left. The nursery waiting lists in the main residential communities run to several months.

Frequently asked questions

How much does giving birth cost in Dubai?
A normal delivery in a private hospital runs AED 15,000 to AED 30,000, and a caesarean AED 25,000 to AED 45,000, before any complications or neonatal care. Government hospitals charge substantially less to residents with a Dubai Health Authority card. Insurance is mandatory for residents, but maternity cover usually requires a waiting period of six to twelve months.
Is maternity insurance compulsory in the UAE?
Health insurance is compulsory for all Dubai residents and employers must provide it, but the level of maternity cover varies enormously between plans. Basic mandatory plans cover antenatal care and delivery with co-payments and annual caps that a complicated birth exceeds quickly. Checking the maternity sub-limit and the waiting period before conceiving is what avoids a large bill.
What paperwork does a newborn need in the UAE?
A birth notification from the hospital, then a birth certificate within 30 days, attestation by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, a passport from your own embassy, an Emirates ID and a residence visa, all within 120 days of birth. Fines accrue after that. The sequence matters, since each document is required for the next.
How much is childcare and schooling in Dubai?
Nursery places run AED 2,500 to AED 6,000 a month for full time, and a live-in nanny costs AED 3,000 to AED 5,000 a month plus visa sponsorship, accommodation and flights. School fees start around AED 25,000 a year and exceed AED 100,000 at the most established institutions, which is the single largest long-term cost for expatriate families.
Can a baby born in the UAE get citizenship?
No. The UAE grants nationality by descent rather than by place of birth, so a child born to expatriate parents takes the parents' nationality and needs a residence visa like any other resident. The visa is sponsored by whichever parent holds the residence permit, and must be in place within 120 days of birth.
Does maternity leave exist in the UAE private sector?
Yes: 60 days, of which 45 are at full pay and 15 at half pay, for employees who have completed a year of service, with reduced entitlement below that. Fathers receive five days of parental leave within six months of the birth. Many multinational employers offer more, and the gap between the legal minimum and the contract is worth checking before planning.
