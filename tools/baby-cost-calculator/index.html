Baby Cost Calculator

Published by Radif Partners · Updated 21 September 2026

This calculator estimates what a baby will cost you in the first year, using your country, your state or region, the childcare you intend to use and how you plan to feed. It exists because the widely quoted figures do not survive contact with an individual family: infant childcare varies by a factor of four between American states, an insured birth costs between nothing and $8,000 out of pocket depending on the plan and the month, and formula costs $55 or $250 a month for the same regulated composition. Everything runs in your browser, nothing you enter is transmitted or stored on a server, and there is no account to create. The figures behind it are the ones documented on the guides linked throughout this site, with the source and the year attached.

Why our figure may differ from one you have seen elsewhere

Published first-year totals vary by a factor of three, and the differences are almost always definitional rather than factual. Some include childcare and some do not, which alone accounts for up to $24,000. Some count the marginal housing cost of an extra room and some treat housing as a household expense. Some include lost earnings from leave, which is income forgone rather than money spent and is counted here separately. Some quote the cost to a two-parent middle-income household in a specific year and are then repeated without adjustment for inflation or for a different family.

This calculator counts spending only, for the first twelve months, for the household you describe. It includes the birth, childcare from the month you say it starts, feeding, nappies, equipment, clothing and routine healthcare. It excludes housing, lost earnings and anything beyond twelve months. Where you compare the result with another source, check those four boundaries before concluding that either figure is wrong.

Privacy

Nothing you enter here leaves your device. The calculation runs in JavaScript in your own browser, there is no server-side processing, and no salary, postcode or due date is transmitted anywhere. Where the tool remembers your answers between visits it uses your browser's local storage, which only this site can read on your own machine and which you can clear at any time from your browser settings. The privacy policy sets out what the site does collect, which is anonymous audience measurement and nothing else.

What the calculator asks, and why

  • Country. It determines whether the birth is a cost at all, what leave pays, and which benefits and tax provisions apply. The three systems covered, the United States, the United Kingdom and the UAE, behave so differently that a single model would be wrong for all three.
  • State or region. The largest single driver of the total after the childcare decision itself. An infant place costs $6,300 a year in Mississippi and over $24,000 in Massachusetts, and metropolitan areas run well above their own state figure.
  • Childcare type. Nursery, a home-based provider, a nanny, family care or none. The gap between the cheapest and most expensive arrangement is wider than every other line in the budget combined.
  • Feeding plan. Breastfeeding, formula or mixed. A year of exclusive formula costs $850 to $2,700 depending on brand; breastfeeding costs $150 to $700 in equipment. Mixed feeding sits proportionally between the two.
  • Gear approach. New, second-hand or a mix. This moves the total by $800 to $2,000, which matters but less than the three answers above.

How to read the result

The figure returned is a first-year total for the household, not a monthly average, and it is a range rather than a point. Treat the low end as what the year costs if nothing goes wrong and you make the cheaper choice at each fork; treat the high end as the realistic planning figure. Most families land closer to the middle, and the ones who land at the low end are usually those with family childcare or a long period of parental leave.

Three things the result deliberately does not include. It excludes lost earnings from leave or reduced hours, which is a separate calculation done properly in the return-to-work guide, because counting it inside a spending total double-counts the same decision. It excludes the marginal housing cost of an extra room, which varies too much by market to model usefully. And it excludes anything after the first twelve months; the birth-to-eighteen breakdown covers that.

What to do with the number

  1. Split it into the birth and the rest. The birth cost has a date attached and should be saved for specifically, by automated transfer, completing a month before the due date.
  2. Subtract what you will get back. A dependent care FSA, an HSA, the Child Tax Credit and any state subsidy together return $2,000 to $6,000 to a typical American household. Run the result past the tax benefits guide before deciding the number is final.
  3. Test the childcare answer. Re-run with a different childcare type and with the start month pushed back. Those two changes usually move the total more than everything else you could do.
  4. Check it against the monthly view. The budget planner takes your income and fixed costs and shows what is left each month, which is the question most households actually need answered.

Where the underlying figures come from

Childcare rates are state-level figures for licensed centre-based and home-based care, cross-checked against the federal affordability benchmark. Birth costs are typical insured out-of-pocket amounts for vaginal and caesarean delivery, with the uninsured billed range shown separately. Formula prices are per-ounce costs for the brands listed on the formula cost page, applied to intake by age. Equipment and consumable costs are published retail prices collected from national retailers and stated as ranges across brands. Every one of those is set out with its source on the guide it comes from, and the methodology page explains how ranges are built and how older figures are restated in current prices.

The calculator is updated when an official schedule changes and reviewed annually for market prices. If a figure looks wrong for your area, it may well be: write to us with the page and the number and we will check it against the source.

Frequently asked questions

What does this calculator include?
One-off equipment, recurring supplies, healthcare and childcare, adjusted for the country and the choices you enter: breastfeeding or formula, daycare or a nanny, new or second-hand equipment. It excludes housing changes and lost income during leave, which are larger than everything else combined for many families but depend entirely on circumstances.
Why is the range so wide?
Because childcare varies by a factor of three between US states, and because the choice between formula and breastfeeding, or between a nanny and daycare, changes the annual figure by five figures. A single national average hides both, which is why the calculator asks rather than assumes.
Are the figures per child or per household?
Per child, and a second child costs roughly 60 to 75 per cent of the first because housing is already paid for and equipment is reused. The exception is two children under three in paid childcare at once, which is close to double and is the most expensive period most families will face.
How often are the figures updated?
At least yearly, and whenever a published source changes: the USDA family expenditure figures, state childcare surveys, and formula and nappy prices tracked from major retailers. Each page carries the date of its last check rather than the date it was served.
