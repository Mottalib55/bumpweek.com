Parental Leave Guide: What You're Entitled To

The US is the only wealthy country with no federal paid parental leave. What you get depends on your state, your employer, and how well you negotiate. Here's a complete breakdown of your rights and how to maximize your time off.

FMLA — what it actually covers

  • 12 weeks unpaid, job-protected leave. Your employer must hold your position (or an equivalent one) while you're on leave, but they are not required to pay you during that time.
  • Only applies to employers with 50+ employees within 75 miles. Small businesses are exempt from FMLA requirements entirely.
  • You must have worked there for 12 months and 1,250 hours. Both conditions must be met. Part-time workers who haven't logged enough hours won't qualify.
  • Covers both mothers and fathers, birth and adoption. FMLA is gender-neutral. It applies to the birth of a child, placement of an adopted or foster child, and care for a spouse, child, or parent with a serious health condition.
  • "Unpaid" means your employer must hold your job but doesn't have to pay you. You will not receive a paycheck during FMLA leave unless your employer voluntarily provides paid leave or you use accrued time off.
  • You can use accrued PTO/sick days during FMLA. Many employees stack their paid time off on top of FMLA to receive at least partial pay during their leave. Your employer may also require you to use PTO concurrently.
  • Your health insurance continues during FMLA. Your employer must maintain your group health coverage under the same terms as if you were still working. However, you still pay your share of the premiums.
  • About 44% of US workers don't qualify for FMLA due to employer size or tenure requirements. If you work for a small company or haven't been there long enough, you may have no federal leave protection at all.

How to negotiate more leave

  1. Start early. Begin the conversation 4-6 months before your due date. This gives your employer time to plan coverage and shows that you're thinking about the team, not just yourself.
  2. Know your company's actual policy. Read the employee handbook. Ask HR for the specific policy in writing. Some companies offer more than the legal minimum but don't advertise it.
  3. Research your industry's norms. Tech companies average 16-20 weeks paid. Finance averages 12-16 weeks. Retail and hospitality often offer only the legal minimum. Knowing what competitors offer gives you leverage.
  4. Propose a phased return. Instead of asking for more weeks off, ask for a gradual return: 2 weeks at 50%, 2 weeks at 75%, then full time. This is easier for managers to approve because it reduces the abrupt transition.
  5. Combine leave types. Stack short-term disability (6-8 weeks for vaginal birth, 8 weeks for C-section) + FMLA + company parental leave + PTO for maximum time off. Map out exactly how each week is covered.
  6. Put it in writing. Document everything. Email summaries after verbal conversations. Get the approved plan in writing from HR. This protects you if there's a management change or a dispute later.
  7. Have a coverage plan ready. Managers worry about workload. Present a plan for how your responsibilities will be covered. Name specific colleagues who can handle key tasks. This removes the biggest objection to granting more leave.

Sample leave request email

Subject: Parental Leave Plan — [Your Name]

Hi [Manager],

I'm excited to share that my partner and I are expecting a baby, with an estimated due date of [date].

I'd like to discuss my parental leave plan. Based on our company policy and FMLA, I'm planning to take [X weeks] beginning approximately [date]. Here's my proposed plan for coverage during that time:

  • [Project A]: [Colleague name] has agreed to handle day-to-day decisions
  • [Project B]: I'll complete [milestone] before my leave and [colleague] will take over
  • [Ongoing responsibilities]: I'll create documentation and transition notes by [date]

I'd also like to discuss the possibility of [a phased return / an additional X weeks / working remotely for the first 2 weeks back]. I believe this would help ensure a smooth transition for both the team and my family.

Can we schedule a time to discuss this in the next two weeks?

Thank you,
[Your Name]

The financial impact of unpaid leave

  • 12 weeks unpaid at $60,000 salary = $13,846 in lost income. That's roughly a quarter of your annual take-home pay. For many families, this creates real financial stress on top of an already expensive time.
  • Start saving early. Put aside $1,000-$1,500/month starting in early pregnancy. By the time the baby arrives, you'll have a cushion to cover living expenses during unpaid leave.
  • Some states allow you to collect short-term disability payments during maternity leave. This typically covers 60-70% of your salary for 6-8 weeks. Check whether your state or employer offers short-term disability insurance.
  • Check if your partner's employer has a better leave policy. Sometimes it makes sense for the higher-earning partner to take less leave, or for the partner with better benefits to take the primary leave. Run the numbers for your specific situation.
