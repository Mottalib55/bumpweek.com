How Much Does a Baby Really Cost?

Published by Radif Partners · Updated 21 September 2026

A baby costs between $21,000 and $50,000 in the first year in the United States, £4,500 to £13,000 in the United Kingdom, and AED 50,000 to AED 90,000 in Dubai. The spread inside each of those ranges is wider than the gap between countries, and it is driven by three things: where you live, what childcare you use, and what your health cover actually pays. Birth costs an insured American family $2,800 to $8,000 and a British family nothing. Infant daycare runs from $6,300 to $24,000 a year depending on the state. Formula costs $55 or $250 a month for the same regulated composition. BumpWeek sets out what each of those lines really costs, with the source and the year attached, and gives you the calculators to apply them to your own situation rather than to a national average.

Start with the three numbers that decide your first year

Whatever country you are in, the first-year total is mostly determined by three figures, and every other decision is small by comparison.

  1. What the birth costs you. In the United States this is your out-of-pocket maximum rather than your deductible, because most families reach it in a birth year, and it is also the cap on a neonatal admission. In the United Kingdom it is effectively zero. In the UAE it is whatever sits above your policy's maternity ceiling and co-payment. Our guide to the cost of giving birth works through all three.
  2. What childcare costs where you live. The single largest line for any family returning to work before the child turns one, and the one with the widest regional spread. The daycare cost map gives the figure for each American state, and the childcare comparison sets nursery, childminder, nanny, nanny share and au pair against each other on true annual cost.
  3. How much leave you can afford to take. The gap between your leave entitlement and your salary, month by month. In the United States that means the FMLA, any state scheme and your employer's policy; in the United Kingdom, Statutory Maternity Pay and anything enhanced on top. The parental leave guide compares them.

Get those three right and the rest, equipment, clothing, feeding, nappies, moves the total by a few thousand at most. Get them wrong and no amount of careful shopping compensates.

What to do, and when

First trimester. Read your health cover and write down the deductible, the out-of-pocket maximum and whether the newborn has a separate one. Find out what leave you are entitled to from all three sources. Join the childcare waiting lists you are considering, because infant places in high-demand areas run six to twelve months out and joining costs nothing. Start the pre-baby financial checklist.

Second trimester. Have the leave conversation with your employer while there is still time to plan cover. Request the hospital's good-faith estimate in writing. Write a will and name a guardian. Buy the durable equipment second-hand, unhurried. Start the automated transfer into the birth-cost pot.

Third trimester. Elect the dependent care FSA and confirm the HSA contribution at open enrolment, or diarise the window after the birth. Confirm how and when the newborn is added to the policy. Set up the direct debits, because nothing gets organised in the first eight weeks.

After the birth. Register the birth, add the baby to the policy, file the claims. Revisit the budget at three months, not before, budgets written in the first fortnight are always wrong.

Three countries, three different problems

The United States. The birth and childcare are both uncapped, and there is no federal paid leave. The levers that matter are the insurance review, the pre-tax accounts, and the state's childcare subsidy thresholds, which are usually higher than families assume. See the US cost guide and the tax benefits guide.

The United Kingdom. The birth is free and the statutory pay is long but thin. The problems are the deadlines, the fifteenth week notification, the three-month backdating limit on Child Benefit, applying for funded hours the term before, and the drop in income from week seven. See the UK money guide.

The UAE. Health cover is mandatory but maternity is frequently capped, excluded or subject to a waiting period, and there is no state child benefit at all. Leave is short, so childcare must be arranged before the baby is three months old. See the Dubai guide.

How the figures here are produced

Every number on this site carries the source it came from and the year it describes. Official statistics come first, the USDA expenditure study, the Consumer Expenditure Survey, the Office for National Statistics, and anything set by a rule, such as a tax credit or a statutory payment, is copied from the government schedule rather than estimated. Where no public body measures a market, such as nanny rates, we use sector surveys that disclose their sample and their date, and we say so.

Costs are published as ranges rather than averages, because an average hides the thing that matters: infant childcare varies by a factor of four across American states, and the national mean describes almost nobody. Older statistics are restated in current prices and we say when we have done so. Nothing here is financial, tax or medical advice, and where a figure on this site and an official schedule disagree, the official schedule governs. The full account is on the methodology page, and corrections go to the address on the legal notice.

The savings that are actually worth making

A first-year budget can be cut by 30 to 40 per cent without buying anything worse, and the order matters more than the effort. The two pre-tax accounts, a dependent care FSA and an HSA, take twenty minutes at open enrolment and return $2,000 to $4,000 a year, which is more than every shopping decision combined. Buying the cot, pushchair, carrier and high chair second-hand saves $800 to $2,000 and costs one weekend. Choosing a store-brand formula saves $900 to $1,400 over a year for a product regulated to the same federal standard as the premium brand beside it on the shelf. Claiming the credits and benefits you qualify for returns $1,000 to $3,000.

Three things are worth full price. A car seat should be bought new, because the crash history of a used one is unknown and the shell degrades with age. A cot mattress should be new and firm. And health cover should be chosen on the family out-of-pocket maximum and the network rather than on the monthly premium, because a plan costing $80 a month more can be $4,000 cheaper across a birth year. The money-saving guide ranks the rest by return.

Questions this site answers

  • What will the birth cost me? Between $2,800 and $8,000 out of pocket for an insured American family, nothing on the NHS, and whatever exceeds your policy ceiling in the UAE. The variables are the deductible reset date, the delivery method and whether every provider is in network.
  • Can we afford for one of us to stay home? The year-one arithmetic often says yes and the lifetime arithmetic usually says the four-day compromise is better. The return-to-work guide runs both calculations.
  • How much should we have saved before the birth? The expected out-of-pocket birth cost first, then one month of expenses, then the unpaid portion of leave. Three months of post-baby expenses is the full target, usually $8,000 to $15,000.
  • What does raising a child cost in total? $237,000 to $370,000 to eighteen in the United States, £160,000 to £220,000 in the United Kingdom, university excluded in both. The full breakdown covers seven countries.
  • What does IVF cost if we need it? $12,000 to $25,000 a cycle in the United States before medication, and most patients need more than one. The IVF guide covers the add-ons that quoted prices exclude.

Use the calculators, not the averages

Every tool here runs entirely in your browser: nothing you type is sent anywhere, and there is no account to create. The baby cost calculator asks for your country, your state or region, your childcare plan and your feeding plan, and returns a first-year figure built from those answers rather than a national mean. The budget planner works the other way round, starting from your income and fixed costs and showing what is left each month once the baby's lines are added. The registry checklist tracks what you already have against what a newborn actually needs, with a price against each item so the list is a budget rather than a wish.

If you only do one thing on this site, run the cost calculator with your own postcode and childcare plan, then compare the result with the headline figure you arrived with. The difference between the two is the reason this site exists.

What we do not cover

There is no medical content on this site and no advice on how to care for a child. There is no financial advice in the regulated sense either: the figures describe what typical arrangements cost, not what you should do with your money, and where a decision turns on your own tax position, insurance contract or employment terms we say so and point to the official source that governs it. Nothing here accounts for an individual case, and where a figure on this site and an official schedule disagree, the schedule governs.

We also do not cover every country. The guides are built for the United States, the United Kingdom and the Gulf, because those are the three systems we can keep current to the standard the rest of the site claims. Figures for other countries appear in comparison tables, clearly labelled as such, and are not maintained to the same frequency.
